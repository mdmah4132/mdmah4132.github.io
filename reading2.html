The reading and video highlighted an important cost-benefit compromise to LLM usage effort and convenience. This study led me to think about other "invisible" costs of LLM usage in education, especially the dichotomy between think with the help of LLMs and think for the sake of using LLMs. In academic environments, LLMs can be used to promote thinking and to provide comfort when accomplishing tasks. Yet, when they are used excessively, they can inhibit thinking and lessen the amount of thinking effort required to learn, especially when it becomes routine.

In fact, the researchers could see actual differences in neural connectivity for participants depending on what condition they had been in. The lowest connectivity was in the condition in which participants used LLMs to write their essays, and the highest was in the condition in which participants used the fewest outside resources. This supports an important concept that when we delegate a thinking task to an LLM, our own brains are less involved in that task-and that kind of indirect training appears to occur in our neural functioning, too.

The other thing that really made me think was a kind of trend I saw for ownership and recall. The participants who did the least amount of work writing their essays, which means doing less thinking and writing, said that they felt a much greater sense of ownership over the essay, but actually had difficulty retrieving or citing examples from the essay later. The ones who did the most work in producing the essays said they didn't feel as much ownership over their essays, but then remembered so much more and were able to reference the essay better when they were asked to. What they actually remembered to refer to seems the opposite of the feeling of ownership.

However, I'm not sure this necessarily implies that LLMs are all that bad for learning. It's quite different to use an LLM to think for yourself than it is to use them to refine your own thinking. A student might, for example, go away and generate their own argument, but then use the LLM to attack it-asking for weaknesses, counter-arguments, or alternative lines of thought. In this way, the LLM is just used to reflect and revise, rather than to generate-ultimately the process still begins with the student, only the LLM is there to kickstart that process and take it further.

In sum, I think this discussion clarified for me that the hidden price of convenience is at the very least a lack of deep learning. I don't want to rule out LLMs, but I want to use them purposefully, deliberately, in ways that keep my thinking at the forefront, not in the background, of any task. For tasks where the final product includes thinking that will improve writing, memory, or critical thinking skills, I want to ensure that I have thought through the problem for myself first, then use the LLM to add, critique, challenge, or extend that thinking, not to inspire the initial thought.
